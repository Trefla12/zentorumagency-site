The anatomy of a landing page that converts

A landing page has one job. Here is every section it needs, in order, with plain-English advice and a checklist so you can build or fix yours.

By Zentorum Agency5 min readUpdated 1 Nov 2026

A landing page is a page with one job. You send people to it from an ad, an email, a social post or a search result, and you want them to do one thing: call, book, sign up or buy.

Unlike a homepage, it does not try to show everything. It focuses. Here are the parts that make a landing page work, in the order visitors meet them.

First, pick one goal

Before any design, decide what a successful visit looks like. One goal. Not "learn about us, follow us and buy". Just one: "Request a quote", for example.

Every element on the page should help that goal. If it does not, remove it.

1. The headline

This is the most-read line on the page. It should say what you offer and who it is for, in plain words. A clear headline beats a clever one.

  • Say the benefit, not just the feature.
  • Match the words of the ad or link that brought the visitor.
  • Keep it short enough to read in a glance on a phone.

For example: "A new website for your business in under two weeks" is clearer than "Welcome to the future of web".

2. The sub-headline

One or two lines that explain how or give more detail. Think of it as the answer to "What exactly do you do for me?"

3. The main call to action

Put your button where people can see it without scrolling. Use words that say what happens: "Get my free quote", "Book a call", "Start my order". "Submit" tells people nothing.

Repeat the same button further down so people can act whenever they are ready. Use the same action each time. Do not offer five different options.

4. A visual that shows the offer

A real photo, a clear product shot, a short video or a simple graphic. It should help the visitor picture the result. Avoid generic images that could belong to any business. If you sell a product, renders and photos both have their place.

Keep file sizes small so the page loads quickly. Slow pages lose visitors before they read a word.

5. The problem and the solution

Briefly describe the problem your visitor has, in their own words. Then show how you solve it. Two or three short paragraphs or bullets are enough.

6. Benefits, not just features

A feature is what something is. A benefit is what it does for the customer. "24-hour reply" is a feature. "You never wait days to hear back" is a benefit. List three to five benefits in simple language.

7. Proof

People want to know others have been happy. Use real proof only:

  • Genuine customer quotes, with permission.
  • Reviews. Learn how to earn them in how to get more Google reviews.
  • Logos of real clients you can name.
  • Photos of your work.
  • Guarantees you actually offer.

Never invent testimonials, numbers or results. They are easy to spot and they damage trust.

8. How it works

Three simple steps make the next move feel easy. For example: tell us what you need, we send a plan, you approve it. Fear of the unknown stops many people from acting. Showing the path removes it.

9. Pricing or what to expect

If you can, give a starting price or a range. People often leave when they cannot tell if they can afford you. If prices vary, say what drives the difference, and what is included. Be clear about timing too.

10. Answers to common questions

A short FAQ handles doubts before they stop the sale. Cover cost, time, what you need to provide, and what happens if they are not happy. Keep answers short and honest.

11. The form

If you collect details, ask only what you need. A name, an email or phone number and a short message are often enough. Every extra field reduces the number of people who finish. Show what happens next: "We reply within 6 hours."

12. A final call to action

End with a clear repeat of the offer and the button. Many visitors read to the bottom and want to act right there.

What to leave out

  • A full navigation menu that lets people wander away.
  • Long about-us stories.
  • Several competing offers.
  • Autoplay sound.
  • Pop-ups that block the page, especially on phones.

Keep a simple logo and perhaps a contact link, and that is all.

Design basics that help

  • Whitespace: give elements room so the eye knows where to go.
  • Contrast: your button should be the most noticeable item.
  • Consistency: use your brand colors and fonts. See how to choose brand colors.
  • Mobile first: most visitors may be on phones. Read why your phone visitors matter most.

Test and improve

A landing page is never really finished. Once you have visitors, watch what happens.

  1. Measure the share who take your main action.
  2. Change one thing at a time: the headline, the button words or the image.
  3. Give each change enough time and visitors for a fair comparison.
  4. Keep what works and drop what does not.

Avoid promising results. No page converts for everyone, and what works for one business may not work for another.

Landing page checklist

  • One clear goal.
  • Headline that names the benefit and the audience.
  • Button above the fold with specific words.
  • Real image or video of the offer.
  • Benefits in simple language.
  • Honest proof.
  • Three-step process.
  • Price range or expectations.
  • Short FAQ.
  • Short form with a promise of what happens next.
  • Fast load and clean on mobile.

Quick questions

What is a landing page?

A landing page is a single page built for one goal, such as booking a call or buying one product. It usually has no distracting menu and one main call to action.

How is a landing page different from a homepage?

A homepage introduces the whole business and serves many visitors. A landing page speaks to one audience with one offer and one next step.

How do I know if my landing page works?

Track the share of visitors who take your main action, such as submitting the form. Change one thing at a time and compare results over a fair period.
